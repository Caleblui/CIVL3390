3 · The two floor systems

Band beam & slab vs flat slab & drop panel · Phase 1 & 2

Band beam & slab

  • Slab: 1 m strip, multi-span, one-way to the bands. Shear reinforcement is almost never needed (check V* ≤ φVuc). Crack control often governs the steel (yours: N12-150 T&B).
  • Band: carries half the slab span each side (design strip = L/2 + L/2). End spans and cantilevers govern. Always has ties: they're shear steel and hold the flexural bars in place.
  • Lef = lesser of L (c/c) or Ln + D (cl 6.2.2). Ductility: kuo ≤ 0.36.

Flat slab & drop panels

  • Design strip each way (half-way to adjacent columns), split into a column strip and two half middle strips.
  • Mo = FdLtLo²/8 (cl 6.10.4). Course split: −ve 0.75 column strip / 0.25 middle strip; +ve 0.5 / 0.5.
  • Drop panel extends ≥ L/6 each side of the column centreline, so ≈ L/3 square. Depth ≈ 1.75 × slab. Its job: punching shear plus the extra depth for hogging moment.
  • Punching: cl 9.3.3 (M*v = 0, pinned columns) vs cl 9.3.4 (with M*v, fixed columns). The moment transfer cut your capacity by ~37 %.
Punching (AS 3600 cl 9.3): φVuo = φ·u·dom·fcv fcv = 0.17(1 + 2/βh)√f'c ≤ 0.34√f'c u = critical perimeter at dom/2 from the column face; βh = long/short side of column; φ = 0.75 With moment transfer (cl 9.3.4): Vu = Vuo / [1 + u·M*v / (8·V*·a·dom)]
Your Phase 1 cost result: band beam $435/m² vs flat slab $447/m², only 2.5 % apart. That's within the accuracy of a preliminary estimate. The saving was concrete volume (0.276 vs 0.306 m³/m²). Formwork is dearer for bands (both faces of each stem).

Exam lines

  • If the long span is ≥ 1.5 × the short span, a band beam and slab is probably better. Flat slabs suit square grids.
  • The simplified method (cl 6.10) needs ≥ 2 spans, adjacent spans within 20 % (ratio ≤ 1.2), Q ≤ 2G, and uniform load. Yours failed (1.55), so Phase 2 used frame analysis.

4 · Analysis ideas the exam loves

Pattern loading · fixed vs pinned columns · ductility & redistribution · idealised frame

Pattern loading (2023 Q2(d), 5 marks)

  • What: live load isn't always everywhere. Arrange Q on alternate spans (max positive midspan moment) and on adjacent spans (max negative moment at the shared support), as well as all spans (max reactions and column loads).
  • Applies to: continuous (statically indeterminate) beams, slabs and floor frames under live load. Dead load is always on every span.
  • Why it matters: the full-load case under-predicts midspan sagging and some support hogging. It can also produce moment reversal, so reinforcement is needed where full load says none is. The cl 6.10 coefficients (wL²/11, wL²/10…) already include pattern effects, which is why they don't add up to wL²/8.

Columns fixed vs pinned to the floors (Phase 2)

  • You analysed twice: a 2-storey 2D frame with columns fixed at the floors and pinned at the footing, then a continuous beam on pinned columns.
  • Your band beam: fixed columns cut the 10.2 m midspan sag by 32 % (520 vs 766 kNm) and put up to 562 kNm into the upper columns. The pinned model gave the larger beam moments, so you designed the floor from it.
  • Why that's allowed: RC is ductile. If a column gets a moment it wasn't designed for, local cracking and yielding "release" it, and the moment redistributes to the floor that was designed for it. "Ductility is truly our best friend." It only works for a braced frame, where walls carry the lateral load. In an unbraced frame (Conifer Knoll centre) the column moments are essential.
  • Footings are pinned because small footing rotations and settlement release the base moment.

Moment redistribution (cl 6.2.7)

In indeterminate structures you can adjust elastic moments, usually by up to ~15 %, provided ductility (kuo limits) and static equilibrium are maintained. It's used to shave peak support moments to get a nicer bar layout. The limits exist to control cracking.

Cracked section stiffness (Table 6.2.4)

For the linear elastic analysis, beams use ~0.4Ig and columns ~0.8Ig. ULS deflections from the computer aren't serviceability deflections: correct by load-factor ratio and Ief/I, then multiply long-term by kcs.

Exam lines

  • "For a braced frame, gravity-induced column moments are small and don't change the floor design. Designing the floor as continuous over pinned columns is safe because ductile RC redistributes any unintended column moment."
  • "Model on centrelines, then reduce moments and shears to the support face."

5 · Lateral stability and shear walls

Braced frame · rule of 10 · shear centre · why "shear" wall
  • Braced frame: stair, lift and added walls are far stiffer than the columns, so they take all the lateral load (wind, EQ, robustness). The columns are then "gravity only", pinned top and bottom.
  • Rule of 10: if Kwalls ≥ 10 × ΣKcolumns, ignore the columns. K ∝ EI/L and E, L are the same, so compare I. One 200 × 5400 wall: I = 200·5400³/12 = 2624 × 10⁹ mm⁴ = 486 × a 300 × 600 column (strong axis). For 15 columns in half the building, Lwall,min = 3.65 m, which fits in a parking bay.
  • Balance and torsion: lateral load acts at the centre of mass (EQ) or centre of pressure (wind). If the walls' shear centre isn't on that line, the floor twists in plan and the walls take extra torsional shears. Add walls "thoughtfully" so the arrangement is symmetric (bisymmetric: shear centre = centroid).
  • Planar walls are stiff in-plane but flexible out-of-plane. They act as shear walls in one direction and as "pin-jointed" (they flex) for floor movement perpendicular to them. That's how they let the floor shrink.
  • Low rise: treat walls as equally stiff, distribute by statics. High rise: deformations matter, so calculate the shear centre.
  • Wall design (AS 3600 Sec 11): vertical load from tributary floor plus self weight, at minimum eccentricity (cl 11.5.4). In-plane shear: V* ≤ φVu = φ(Vuc + Vus). Minimum crack-control steel usually governs. Check overturning and tension at one end: your SW-1 self weight couldn't resist overturning, so it needs a tied strip footing or tension piles.
2023 Q2(b): "Why is the term shear wall used?" Because the wall resists lateral load in its own plane, mainly by in-plane shear. It's a deep vertical cantilever (short height to length), so shear deformation is comparable to bending deformation (δ = PL³/3EI + 6PL/5AG). It's the "web" that carries diaphragm shear from each floor down to the footing, much like shear in a beam web. Squat walls behave like strut and tie. Out-of-plane it's weak and flexible.

Exam lines

  • "Stiff elements attract load in proportion to stiffness; the walls are 400+ times stiffer than a column, so the columns can be ignored for lateral load."
  • "Floors act as rigid horizontal diaphragms that deliver the lateral load to the walls."

6 · Movement joints, corbels and strut-and-tie

The single most examined concept · 2023 Q1 was entirely this

Why joints: concrete floors shrink as they dry (initial drying shrinkage), creep and shorten axially under load, and expand or contract with temperature and moisture forever. A 55–65 m floor tied to stiff walls at both ends can't move, so restraint stresses crack it. A movement joint splits the floor into independent portions (your MJ was on grid E, aligned on both floors).

Joint types

  • Double column: two columns side by side, each floor portion stands alone.
  • Column / wall corbel: a nib cast on the column or wall face, with the floor on a bearing pad.
  • Half (dowel) joint in a beam: two "corbels" overlapped. Put it near the quarter point of a band span (low moment, moderate shear). Yours: half joint at grid E.
  • Edge beam with corbel along a column line (flat slab option).
  • Dowel joint: smooth dowels debonded or sleeved on one side. Transfers shear, allows slip along the dowel.
  • Temporary joints / pour (delay) strips: let early shrinkage happen, then infill.

Strut and tie (AS 3600 Sec 7)

  • Used in D-regions (disturbed, within about D of a discontinuity) where plane sections don't stay plane: corbels, half joints, pile caps, squat walls.
  • Steps: model lines (truss), solve forces by trig, give struts width, check strut stress (efficiency βs, cl 7.2), size the tie Ast = T*/(φfsy) (φ = 0.8), check nodes (cl 7.4), anchor the ties by the node, add bursting and crack-control steel (cl 7.2.4, 8.6).
  • Prismatic struts are rare; bottle-shaped is the norm; fan-shaped spread in footings. Hydrostatic node: faces normal to the forces, equal stress.
  • Corbel at a beam bottom needs suspension reinforcement (ties) to hang the strut force back up into the beam.

Where to put joints and walls together (the crux)

  • Each portion of floor must be laterally stable on its own: walls in both directions, balanced.
  • A floor shrinks towards a "fixed point", the stiffness centroid of its walls. Put the restraint near the centre of each portion, so shrinkage moves away from it freely. Never put stiff walls in the same direction at both ends of a portion: they'd fight the shrinkage and crack the floor.
  • Where a core sits at a far end, either isolate it (corbel/slip joint: it then carries vertical load only) or make the core walls planar and pinned out of plane.
  • Ground slab on ground: separate joint layout (saw-cut or construction joints, mesh in top, ~30 cover) to control shrinkage cracking. Isolate it from columns with 10 mm joint filler.

Exam lines

  • "Corbel joints transfer vertical shear through a bearing pad but allow horizontal sliding, so the floor can shrink and expand without restraint cracking."
  • "The corbel must be sized for bars, cover, bend radii and anchorage, with the load point not too close to the edge (shear-friction failure). Start at 200 × 200."

7 · Columns and walls

Minimum eccentricity · biaxial bending · slenderness · vehicle impact · laps
  • Minimum eccentricity (cl 10.1.2): M* ≥ 0.05D·N*, about each axis. Real loads are never perfectly concentric. With pinned-column analysis this is the design moment.
  • Biaxial bending (cl 10.6.4): needed for the flat slab interior column because there are moments from two orthogonal design strips (M*x and M*y). The band beam frame was only analysed in one direction. 2023 Q2(a) The flat-slab-only checks are: biaxial bending of the column, and punching shear of the slab/drop panel around the column with moment transfer.
  • Short vs slender (braced): Le/r ≤ 25 (r = 0.3D for rectangles) means short, no moment magnifier (δb = 1). Lecturer's example: 2300/(0.3·300) = 25.5 ≈ 25, short.
  • Accidental vehicle impact (AS 1170.1 cl 3.8(a)(i)): 30 kN at 0.5 m above floor, a robustness check. Treat the column as pinned top and bottom (worst case). M ≈ 18 kNm, similar in size to the minimum-eccentricity moment. Add it to M*min and check with the T38 column charts. Weak axis governs for a 300 × 600.
  • Reinforcement: 1 % ≤ Asc/Ag ≤ 4 % (≤ 0.5 % for buried piles). Ties ≥ N10, spacing ≤ min(Dc, 15db). Yours: C1 300 × 600, 6-N20, N10 @ 300, laps 800 (40db).
  • Laps: compression lap ≥ Lsy.c (= 0.22fsydb/√f'c ≥ 0.0435fsydb). Starter bars are cogged onto the footing mat, but the cog is not effective in compression, so the straight length above the mat must be ≥ Lsy.c (this sets footing depth!). Horizontal cog into the top floor.

Durability and fire (Phase 1E)

  • Exposure B1 (near-coastal, exterior): min f'c 32 MPa, cover 40 mm (Table 4.10.3.2). Footings cast against ground: 60–75.
  • NCC: carpark Class 7a, ≤ 3 storeys, so Type C construction (open-deck, no FRL needed for the roof steel). FRL = structural adequacy / integrity / insulation, e.g. 120/120/120 (minutes). AS 3600 Sec 5 turns that into minimum b, D and axis distance as. For this building, durability cover (Sec 4) governed over fire.

8 · Footings

Pad footing thickness · bored piles to AS 2159 · pile cap by strut and tie
2023 Q2(c): three requirements that set minimum pad footing thickness: (1) punching shear capacity without shear reinforcement (like the drop panel check, deducting q* inside the perimeter); (2) development length Lsy.c of the column starter bars, straight, above the bottom mat; (3) one-way beam shear without stirrups. Also: cover to ground plus two bar layers, and enough flexural depth (ku ≤ 0.36) with stiffness for the uniform pressure assumption.

Pad footing steps

  1. N from the pinned-base analysis. Use unfactored G + Q against the allowable bearing (200 kPa), or factor the allowable up by the average load factor (e.g. 1.32).
  2. B² ≥ Ns/qa, round up. Then q* = N*/B².
  3. Depth from ①–④. Steel: cantilever moment q*·B·a²/2 at the column face, each way. Check Ast.min and anchorage (cog at edges).
  4. Yours: 2200 × 2500 × 600, N20 @ 250 each way bottom, N25 concrete, cover 60.

Bored piles (AS 2159)

  1. Ed = N*/2 per pile (factored). +20 % for negative skin friction from the loose fill (down-drag as it consolidates).
  2. Rd,g = φg(fbAb + fm,sAs) ≥ Ed. φg = 0.5 (ARR ≈ 3, no load testing). Tuff: fb = 1500 kPa, fm,s = 100 kPa. Ignore shaft friction in the fill.
  3. Spacing ≥ 2.5D (group effects). 75 mm out-of-position tolerance, so M* ≥ max(N*e, 0.05DN*).
  4. Structural: design as a column with ~0.5 % steel (buried), N25, helix ties. Yours: 2 × Ø750 at 2250 c/c, 9.5 m long, 8-N20.
  5. Pile cap = deep beam, so strut and tie: struts ~45°, bottom tie bars over the piles, cogged and anchored past the node.

Settlement, not soil strength, usually governs allowable bearing (20–25 mm total, ≤ 50 % differential). Footings are dug by excavators, not built to watchmaking precision, so be sensibly conservative.

9 · Phase 3: the steel portal roof

Why our frame is unusual · approximate analysis · first vs second order
  • Normal portals: pinned bases, rigid knee and ridge, cross-bracing in the roof and walls. Sway frame in-plane, braced frame out-of-plane. Pinned bases are usually cheaper (small footings).
  • Ours: no walls (natural ventilation) and columns inboard on the concrete columns. Wall bracing would cost parking bays, so the columns have fixed bases and no cross bracing. Out-of-plane each column is a fixed-base cantilever about its weak axis (longitudinal wind), so there's biaxial bending at the base. The concrete floor easily takes the base moment. Yours: 250 × 250 × 12.5 SHS C450L0 columns, 410UB59.7 rafters with 2450-long haunches, 600 × 600 × 32 base plates.
  • Load cases: 1.2G + 1.5Q (UDL); 0.9G + Wu (uplift: the bottom flange of the rafter goes into compression, so fly braces); 1.2G + Wdown; 1.2G + Wlongitudinal. EQ (light roof, horizontal only) never governs. Yours: 9.2 kN.
  • Approximate analysis: turn the indeterminate frame determinate by inserting pins where you expect M = 0 (you must be able to sketch the expected BMD). It gives first member sizes for SpaceGASS and a hand check of the output. Most errors are input errors.
  • Haunch: a cut UB welded under the rafter at each knee, 10–15 % of span. It restores capacity where the knee moment exceeds the rafter's. Same both ends because wind can come from either side.
  • Deflection often governs portals. Yours: ridge sag 48.7 mm vs L/360 = 68 mm, 99 % of Kit's L/500.
2023 Q2(e): second-order effects. What: first-order analysis assumes deflections are small and equilibrium is on the undeformed shape. Second-order analysis puts equilibrium on the deformed geometry, so axial load × lateral deflection adds moment: P-Δ (frame sway) and P-δ (member bow). M = P(e + δ) instead of Pe. Iterative: AS 4100 uses moment amplification factors by hand, software uses "non-linear geometry" (not non-linear material: steel stays elastic, E = 200 GPa). Applies to: compression members and sway (unbraced) frames, i.e. the portal columns and rafters. Was it significant? No. Your PF1 second-order moments were only 2–4 % above first order. The frame is light, short and stiff, with low axial load, so it isn't sway-sensitive. You still designed with the second-order values.

10 · Wind, purlins, bracing, connections

Phase 3 detailing · concept questions
Wind (AS/NZS 1170.2:2021): Vdes = VR·Mc·Md·(Mz,cat·Ms·Mt) IL2 → V500 = 57 m/s (ULS), V25 = 39 m/s (SLS); Region B1; Md = 0.95; Mz,cat = 0.83 (TC3, h<10 m); Ms = Mt = Mc = 1.0 → Vdes,ult = 44.9 m/s, p = 0.5ρV²Cshp = 1.21·Cshp kPa (SLS 0.57·Cshp = 0.47 pult) Free roof (App B.3): Cshp = Cp,n·Ka·Kl; Cp,w / Cp,l combos e.g. (−0.3, −0.4), (0.4, 0) Ka (area reduction): purlin ≈ 10 m² → 1.0; frame > 100 m² → 0.8 Kl = 3.0 local pressure (purlins only) within 0.5a of upwind corners, a = 0.2 × shorter roof dim Frictional drag Cf = 0.01–0.04 (Table B.3, ribs/corrugations across or along the wind)
  • Purlins (roof, perpendicular to fall) and girts (walls) are cold-formed C/Z sections (G450–550, Z350 zinc). Spacing is set by the sheeting span (Trimdek: 1.1 m end, 1.5 m internal). Span = frame spacing. Pick size and bridging together from the Stramit tables (kN/m at ULS).
  • Outward (uplift) capacity governs: the sheeting restrains the top flange only, so under uplift the unrestrained inner flange is in compression and buckles (LTB). Bridging reduces the effective length (max unbraced 20D or 4 m). A ridge tie stops weak-axis sag before sheeting goes on.
  • Z laps, C can't: lapping Z's over the rafters (15 % of span) doubles capacity where continuous-beam support moments are about twice midspan, so smaller purlins. End spans govern: shorten them by 20 % (Table 6) or use a thicker Z in the end spans (Table 7, e.g. Z200 24/19). Yours: Z200-19 lapped, plus extra Z200-15 at 0.7 m in the corner Kl zones.
  • Fly braces: small angles (50 × 50 × 5 EA) from purlin to the rafter's bottom flange. They restrain the compression flange under uplift or near the knees (hogging), cutting the segment length so φMb → φMs.
  • Eaves ties, ridge tie, roof cross-bracing (M24 rods with turnbuckles): small forces, mostly buildability. Together they carry longitudinal drag to the columns.
  • Connections: pin joint: shear and axial only, flanges NOT connected (web cleat, flexible end plate, bolts between flanges). Rigid joint: shear, axial AND moment, flanges MUST be connected (bolted end plate with bolts outside the flanges, or flange plates). Moment goes through the flanges, shear through the web, axial in proportion to area.
  • Cost: steel $4500/t × 1.1 (fly braces, connections); purlins $/m + 15 % laps, × 1.5 for bolts, transport and install, + GST. Yours: $283,594 (≈ $142/m²).

11 · Q3 routine: RC rectangular section, φMu and φVu (AS 3600:2018)

The 20-mark calculation · full worked version in the 2023 tab

The 15-mark calculation · calculators on the next tab
  1. Load path: slab spans one way to the beams parallel to each other. Tributary width = beam spacing. Secondary beams deliver point loads (their reactions) to the primary beam. Primary beam self weight is its own UDL.
  2. Factored loads: 1.2G + 1.5Q (also check 1.35G). G = slab 25t + SDL + beam self weight.
  3. BMD for the primary beam, with moveable point loads placed to maximise moment. Mark segments between lateral restraints (connections of secondary beams). The critical segment has the largest moment.
  4. αm for that segment: αm = 1.7M*m/√(M*2² + M*3² + M*4²) ≤ 2.5 (quarter, mid, three-quarter points of the segment), or Table 5.6.1 (linear: 1.75 + 1.05βm + 0.3βm², βm = −1 for uniform moment → 1.0).

Sketch drills

Frames: reactions, deflected shape, BMD, SFD, AFD

Q3(b) is worth 10 marks for five qualitative sketches. The Week 13 briefing practised exactly this. Use these rules and you can't go far wrong:

  • Supports: fixed = H, V, M; pin = H, V; roller = 1 force. A pin joint inside a frame = zero moment there.
  • Free end / pin / roller: M = 0 unless a moment is applied.
  • Rigid knee: the moment is continuous round the corner (same value, same tension face: inside or outside). The 90° angle is preserved in the deflected shape.
  • UDL on a member: parabolic BMD, linear SFD. Unloaded member: linear BMD, constant SFD.
  • Draw BMD on the tension face and say so. The deflected shape curves away from the tension face; contraflexure where the BMD crosses zero.
  • Max moment where shear = 0.
  • Axial force in one member = shear in the member it frames into (at a knee).
  • A member pinned at both ends with no load on it is a two-force member: the force acts along the line joining its pins, even if the member is bent (an L-shape). That's the trick in 2023 Q3(b).
  • Indeterminate? Stiffer parts attract more load. Fixed base vs pinned: the fixed base carries half the knee moment back (carry-over 0.5).

Q1 (20) · Open stairs + isolated lift: lateral resistance vs shrinkage

Phase 2, flat slab & drop panel option, section between MJ and Campbell Rd

Scenario: stairs are open (no walls), outside the envelope and separated from each floor by a movement joint. The lift shaft is also separated by MJs at each floor. So none of the cores can give lateral resistance any more.

(a) 15 marks: how to give lateral resistance while allowing shrinkage and thermal movement

  • The problem: previously stair and lift walls were the main lateral-load elements. Now each floor portion (MJ to Campbell Rd) is a plate on 300 × 600 columns with nothing stiff. Columns pinned top and bottom can't resist lateral load (robustness 0.015(G + ψcQ), EQ Fi = 0.1Wi).
  • Option A (preferred): add dedicated planar shear walls (200 thick, ~3.65–5.4 m long by the rule of 10) inside the portion: at least two in the E–W direction and two in the N–S direction (or a ≥ 3 wall non-concurrent arrangement), placed in bay ends or dead space so no parking is lost.
  • Locate them about the centre of the portion so their shear centre ≈ the floor's centre of mass (no torsion), and so that walls in the same direction are close together near the portion's middle. The "fixed point" of shrinkage is then central, and the floor shortens freely towards it from both ends. Don't put parallel walls at opposite ends of the portion: they'd restrain shrinkage and crack the slab.
  • Each planar wall is stiff in-plane (carries shear parallel to itself) and flexible out-of-plane. Detail it as effectively pin-jointed for perpendicular movement, so the floor can move across it.
  • The floor acts as a rigid diaphragm delivering load to the walls. Walls are cast integral with the floors (dowels and starter bars) and run down to their own footings, designed for overturning (tension piles or a tied strip footing if needed).
  • Option B: frame action (unbraced frame) like the centre section of Conifer Knoll: rely on column–slab moment fixity. The columns and slab then carry lateral moments (biaxial column design, more punching shear from M*v, second-order sway effects), and you get more steel in the columns. This is viable for low lateral loads but stiffer walls are cleaner.
  • Allowing for movement: keep the central MJ; add pour (delay) strips to let initial drying shrinkage happen before closing; give enough crack-control steel (cl 9.5.3.4, "moderate" degree, partially restrained); isolate the lift from each floor with a corbel joint on bearing pads (it carries vertical load only, no lateral); isolate the stairs similarly; keep the slab on ground isolated from columns.
  • Lift isolation consequence: the lift shaft must now be self-stable (a free-standing cantilever core from its footing). Its own walls resist its own lateral load.

(b) 5 marks: movement joint between lift shaft and suspended floor

Sketch the corbel detail from Lesson 6: a continuous RC nib (≥ 200 × 200) cast on the lift wall face before the floor; top tie bars anchored into the wall with a cog past the bearing; an elastomeric bearing pad (or slip membrane) under the slab edge; a 20–25 mm gap with compressible filler and sealant (fire-rated if an FRL is needed); a steel cover plate for vehicle wheels, fixed on one side only; slab edge bars returned (U-bars) at the free edge. Label "transfers vertical shear only, allows horizontal movement".

Q2 (20) · Five short answers

All in Lessons 4–9

(a) 3 marks: column check for flat slab not needed for band beam

Biaxial bending (AS 3600 cl 10.6.4) of the interior column, with M*x and M*y from the two orthogonal design-strip frame analyses (fixed columns). The band beam was analysed as a frame in one direction only; the slab direction gave no column moment. Related: punching shear around the column with unbalanced moment transfer (cl 9.3.4) governs the drop panel depth. Mention both.

(b) 3 marks: why "shear wall"?

It resists lateral load acting in its own plane primarily by in-plane shear, like the web of a deep vertical cantilever beam. Because it's squat (height ≈ length), shear deformation and shear capacity (V* ≤ φVuc + φVus) are as important as flexure. It transfers floor-diaphragm shear down to the footings.

(c) 4 marks: three requirements setting minimum pad footing thickness

  1. Punching shear capacity without shear reinforcement at dom/2 from the column face (deduct the bearing pressure inside the perimeter).
  2. Development length of the column starter bars in compression, Lsy.c (the cog on the mat isn't effective in compression).
  3. One-way (beam) shear across the full width at do from the column face, without stirrups.
  4. (Bonus) cover against ground (60–75) plus two layers of bars; flexural depth for ductility (ku ≤ 0.36); stiffness for the uniform bearing assumption.

(d) 5 marks: pattern loading

What: arranging live load Q on selected spans (alternate spans, adjacent spans, all spans), with dead load everywhere, to find the worst moments and shears. Applies to: continuous beams, slabs and floor frames with live load (the multi-span band beam, slab strip and flat slab design strips in Phase 2). Why it matters: full load on all spans doesn't give the maximum midspan sagging (alternate spans do) or the maximum hogging at a given support (adjacent spans do). It can cause moment reversal, so it decides bar quantities and cut-off points. Sketch both arrangements and the BMD envelope.

(e) 5 marks: second-order effects

What: extra moments from axial load acting on the deflected shape: P-Δ (frame sway) and P-δ (member curvature). Equilibrium on the deformed geometry rather than the original. Applies to: compression members and sway frames, i.e. the Phase 3 portal (fixed-base unbraced columns, rafters with axial thrust). Done by SpaceGASS "non-linear geometry" (AS 4100 uses moment amplification by hand). Significant? No. Second-order moments were only 2–4 % above first order: a light roof, short stiff columns and low axial loads, so the frame isn't sway-sensitive.

Q3(b) (10) · Frame with a pin at the top-left knee

The two-force L-bent trick
Read Figure 1 carefully: left column fixed at the base and loaded by a horizontal UDL; a pin at the top-left knee; rigid top-right knee; right column pinned at its base. The beam plus right column is an L-shaped member pinned at both ends with no load on it, so it's a two-force member: the force it carries acts along the diagonal from the top-left pin to the bottom-right pin. That gives you every diagram.
Reactions: A (fixed, left base): H_A (←), V_A (↓), M_A. D (pin, right base): force along DB = H_D (←) + V_D (↑), V_D/H_D = h/L Σ horizontal: H_A + H_D = w·h ; Σ vertical: V_A = V_D ; 1 degree indeterminate (the L-bent acts as an elastic prop) Left column AB: propped cantilever with a flexible prop. M = 0 at the pin B, max hogging at the fixed base (tension on the loaded outer face, > wh²/8 because the prop is flexible), sagging region in between; shear = 0 at max sag. Beam BC: M = 0 at B, linear to M_C = H_D·h at C (tension on top/outside). Constant shear V_D. Axial compression H_D. Column CD: M_C at top (tension outside) linear to 0 at D. Constant shear H_D. Axial compression V_D. Column AB: axial TENSION V_A (the L-bent pushes its top up-left). Deflected: AB bows right, B moves right Δ; BC translates right; the L rotates about D; the 90° at C is preserved; free rotation at pin B.
